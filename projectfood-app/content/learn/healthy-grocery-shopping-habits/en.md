Healthy grocery shopping habits do more for the week than any amount of good intention at the table, for one reason: you eat what you buy. What is in the house on shopping day is what gets eaten by Friday, so the supermarket is the one place where you control the cues for the other six days without needing any willpower at 19:00. This article is part of the guide to [healthy eating habits that stick](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits), and it is the shortest route to a wider plate. The vegetables cannot be tasted if they are still in the shop.

## You eat what you buy

The easiest way to resist a food is never to meet it, and the easiest way to eat a plant is to have it within reach when you are hungry. Both decisions are made in the shop, days before the moment they matter. By the time you are standing in the kitchen at 19:00, the week has already been decided by what is on the shelf.

That turns shopping into a habit with unusual reach. One good shop sets up seven days of cues. One poor shop sets up seven days of negotiating with yourself.

## Shop by category, not by recipe

A list written by meals buys the same five dinners every week, because the meals you can think of on a Saturday morning are the meals you already make. A list written by category makes the gaps visible before you leave the house.

The categories that matter for variety:

- Vegetables, at least a few that were not in last week's bag.
- Fruit, in season.
- Beans and lentils, tinned or dried.
- Whole grains beyond bread: oats, barley, brown rice, bulgur.
- Nuts and seeds, a different bag than last time.
- Herbs and spices, fresh or dried, one you usually skip.
- Something fermented: yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, kefir.

Seven lines, and you can see at a glance which of them is empty.

## Different beats more

This is why variety, rather than volume, is the thing to shop for. In the American Gut Project, adults who ate more than 30 different plant types a week had a more diverse gut microbiome than those who ate 10 or fewer, an observation in adults, not a promise (McDonald, 2018). The guideline that follows is 30 different plants a week, and the shop is where the 30 is decided. Three kinds of apple is one plant. An apple, a pear and a plum are three.

## In season is the cheap kind of variety

Buying what is in season does the rotation for you. The shelf in March is not the shelf in September, so a shopper who follows the season eats differently across the year without planning anything. It is also cheaper and it tastes better, which matters for a habit: the reward is in the first bite rather than in the long run.

A short in-season list for your country, on the fridge or in your phone, is enough. You do not need to know the whole calendar, only what is good this month.

## The thin categories: beans, grains, ferments

Most kitchens are full of vegetables and fruit and nearly empty of the other four categories. Beans, whole grains, nuts and seeds, and fermented foods are where the cheap variety lives, because each of them adds a new plant for very little effort: a tin of chickpeas in the soup, oats at breakfast, a handful of seeds on anything, a spoon of sauerkraut on the side.

If the list has one rule, it is this: every shop brings home something from a category that was empty last week.

## One new plant a week

Put one plant in the basket that you have not eaten this month. Not a project, one item. A vegetable you walked past, a grain you have never cooked, a herb you do not know. It costs a euro or two and it guarantees that the week has one new taste in it, which is the whole habit in miniature. How to turn that into the easy version of each meal is in [how to eat more vegetables every day](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits/eat-more-vegetables-every-day).

## The list is the cue

The list is not only a memory aid; it is the cue for the shopping habit itself. Written at the same moment every week, after the Sunday breakfast or before the Saturday shop, it becomes the fixed point the rest hangs on. "After I write the list, I add one new plant" is a stack that holds, for the same reasons as the one after dinner, which is explained in [habit stacking for healthy eating](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits/habit-stacking-healthy-eating).

Two small rules keep the list honest. Write it when you are not hungry, because hunger writes its own. And keep last week's list, because the gaps repeat and the fastest way to see them is to compare.

## What backfires

- Shopping hungry. The aisle decides, not the list.
- Buying for a diet. A kitchen full of things you are supposed to eat and do not like is a kitchen you avoid.
- Buying a month of good intentions. Fresh food that goes off teaches you that buying it was a mistake.
- Ten new plants at once. Nine of them rot, and the lesson is the wrong one.

## How Project Food does this

Project Food is an app on your phone, for you alone or for everyone at your table. Its stats page shows your week by category, so the thin categories are visible before you shop, and the plant list has an "in season" filter that doubles as a shopping list for this month. After the last meal you tap what you tasted, every new plant becomes a card, and the week counts towards 30 different plants. It does not plan meals and it does not tell you what to buy; it shows you what you have been eating, which is enough.

## Worth remembering

- You eat what you buy. The shop sets the cues for the week.
- A list by category, not by meal. Seven lines.
- Variety over volume: different plants, not more of the same.
- In season rotates the plants for you, cheaply.
- Beans, whole grains, nuts, ferments: the empty shelves, and the easy variety.
- One new plant a week. One, not ten.
