Habit stacking for healthy eating is one sentence: after I do something I already do every day, I will do one small thing with food. The old habit becomes the cue, so you never have to remember to start. It is the most reliable tool in the guide to [healthy eating habits that stick](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits), and the most often misused, because people stack a big action on an anchor that is not really daily. This article gives the formula, the stacks that hold at each meal and in the shop, and the one stack that does most of the work. It also explains why "after I get home" has failed everybody at least once.

## The formula

"After I [current habit], I will [new action]." Three things make it work. The current habit happens every day without being decided. The new action is small enough to do on a bad day. And the two are next to each other in time and place, so the first one physically leads to the second.

The research behind it is plain: actions tied to one consistent daily context become automatic with repetition, and the advice written for doctors is to help people choose a specific small behaviour, attach it to a fixed moment, and expect about 10 weeks (Gardner, 2012). The stack is the "fixed moment" part of that advice, done for you by the day you already have.

## Pick an anchor that happens every day

The anchor is the whole trick, and it is where most stacks break. "After I get home from work" sounds daily and is not: some days you come home at 17:00 and some at 21:00, some days you were not at work, some days you stop at the shop first. The cue fires irregularly, so the action does too.

Good anchors share three traits:

- Daily, including weekends.
- At roughly the same time, give or take an hour.
- Already automatic, so nothing has to be remembered.

For food, the strongest anchor most people have is the last meal of the day. It happens daily, it sits in a narrow window, and it is the one moment when you can still remember what you ate today.

## The stack that holds: after the last meal, tap

"After I clear the table from the last meal, I tap what I tasted today." That one stack carries most of the loop. The meal is the cue. The tap takes under a minute. The count going up is the reward. Tomorrow's plate is nudged by what you saw tonight.

It also fixes the thing that sinks food diaries: recall. A diary written at bedtime asks you to reconstruct the day. A tap right after the last meal asks you to look at the table. The question "what did I taste today" has a short answer at 19:30 and a long one at 23:00.

## Stacks for breakfast, lunch and the shop

Once the evening stack runs by itself, a second one can go in front of it. One at a time, one per season.

- After I pour the morning coffee, I put one piece of fruit on the table. The fruit does the rest.
- After I sit down for lunch, I add one thing that is green, from the fridge or a jar. Frozen counts.
- After I write the shopping list, I add one plant I have not eaten this month. The shelf decides the week.
- After I put the pan on, I add one herb or spice I usually skip. Each one counts as a plant.
- After the Sunday shop, I move the vegetables to the shelf you see first. Cues work on you too.

The shopping stack is the one with the longest reach, because it sets the cues for the other six days; it has its own article, [healthy grocery shopping habits](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits/healthy-grocery-shopping-habits).

## Why most stacks fail

Three failures account for nearly all of them. The anchor is not daily, so the cue does not fire. The action is too big, so on a tired day it is skipped and the skip becomes the habit. Or two or three new actions are stacked at once, so none gets the repetitions it needs.

A fourth failure is quieter: the stack has no reward, so it runs on intention for a few weeks and then stops. The tap after dinner has a reward built in, a count that went up. A stack without one needs a tick, a line on a chart, anything that shows the day happened. [Why healthy eating is hard to stick to](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits/why-healthy-eating-is-hard-to-stick-to) covers what the missing reward does to a habit.

## When the anchor moves

Holidays, shift work, a sick week: the anchor moves and the stack goes quiet. That is fine, and the rule is the same as for any miss. Do not start again on Monday; do it at the next meal that happens. A stack is a cue, not a contract, and a cue that fires late is still a cue.

## How Project Food does this

Project Food is an app on your phone, for you alone or for everyone at your table. You tell it when you usually eat the last meal of the day, and about half an hour later it asks one question, what did you taste today. That question is the stack, delivered. You tap the plants, every new one becomes a card, every taste counts, and the streak counts days with at least one plant logged. It never asks a second question.

## Worth remembering

- After I [daily habit], I will [small food action]. That is the whole method.
- The anchor has to be daily, at the same time, and already automatic.
- The last meal of the day is the anchor most people have.
- One action per anchor, one new stack per season.
- A stack needs a reward: a tick, a count, a card.
- When the anchor moves, the next meal is the restart.
