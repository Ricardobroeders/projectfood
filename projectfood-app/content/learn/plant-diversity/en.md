30 plants a week sounds like a project until you look at one evening. Tuesday's pasta already makes 4 or 5: wheat, tomato, onion, garlic, basil. Nobody has ever looked at it that way. This is the family version of the guideline: what the 30 means, what counts, how a household shares one count and how a first week goes. A spreadsheet is the quickest way to make anyone dislike lentils.

## What 30 plants a week means

The guideline comes from the American Gut Project, a large citizen-science study in which adults who ate more than 30 different plants a week had a more varied gut flora than adults who ate 10 or fewer (McDonald, 2018). It is an observation in adults, based on what people reported eating. It says nothing about what the number does for a child, and we do not say it does.

What the number is, is a useful anchor. It points at more different things, not at more food. A household that rotates the same five vegetables sits at about 10 plants. The same household with a new legume and a handful of seeds gets a good way further without one portion getting bigger. That is why we call it a guideline and not a target you hit or miss.

## What counts as a plant

A plant is a plant food you can name. There are eight kinds: fruit, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, herbs and spices. Each kind counts once a week, however often you eat it. Apple on Monday and Wednesday is 1 plant, apple and pear is 2.

The rule is generous on purpose. The point is variety, not bookkeeping. Coffee, a potato, a spoon of ketchup, a pinch of cumin: most of the questions parents ask have a short and friendly answer. The full list, with every borderline case in one place, is in the article on [what counts as a plant](/en/learn/plant-diversity/what-counts-as-a-plant).

## Counting as a family

A household shares one count. You count per kind, not per person. If you eat the lentils and your partner eats them too, that is 1 plant. If your child only licks the lentils, that counts as well, because the household has had them. Nobody has to eat their own 30.

That makes the number reachable in a way it is not for one person alone. Four people with four different plates cover far more kinds than one person trying hard. One eats the olives, another the cucumber, the third tastes the parsley. The list grows and nobody has to see their plate as a score.

How a whole week with four eaters reaches 30 without cooking twice gets its own article.

## The tasting count belongs to the child

There are two counts, and it helps to keep them apart. The household count is the grown-ups': 30 plants a week, together. The tasting count is the child's: how often they have tasted something, on the way to liking it. Most children need 8 to 15 tastes of a new food before they accept it, sometimes more. A lick counts.

So the child is never measured against 30. Their game is smaller and closer: two more tastes and the card for that plant goes to silver. Everything they taste feeds the household count, but the pressure stays with the adults, where it is a cheerful number. How to keep the tasting count without turning it into a project is in the article on [how many times to try a food](/en/learn/learn-to-eat-everything/how-many-times-to-try-a-food).

## What an ordinary week looks like

You do not need to cook anything special. A week of ordinary meals gets closer than you would think, because every meal is made of several kinds.

- **Breakfast.** Porridge oats with an apple, a spoon of seeds and cinnamon: 4 plants.
- **Lunch.** Bread with cucumber and tomato and a handful of nuts: 4 or 5 plants, depending on the bread.
- **Pasta with tomato sauce.** Wheat, tomato, onion, garlic and basil: 5 plants.
- **Soup or stew.** Lentils, carrot, celery, onion and parsley: 5 plants, one of which you have already counted.
- **Rice with chicken and vegetables.** Rice, pepper, broccoli and ginger: 4 plants.
- **In between.** A clementine, a banana, popcorn: 3 plants.

That is an example, not a prescription. Add up a week of meals like these, take out the duplicates and you land somewhere between 18 and 25. The last few come from one new plant at the shop, a herb in the wok or a new legume on Saturday.

## Why variety beats quantity

The count is kinds, not portions. That changes how you look at a week. An extra bowl of broccoli on Thursday adds nothing to the count, and a first spoon of black beans adds 1. For a household that is a relief: nobody has to eat more, you only have to put something different on the table now and then.

It also fits how children learn. A child who gets the same three vegetables every day has few chances to meet anything new. A child who finds one small new kind beside the plate each week builds their list slowly, at their own pace. The household's variety and the child's tasting count pull the same way.

## The shopping rule

The easiest way to grow the list is one question at the shop: which plant have we not had in three weeks. That is the whole rule. Pick that one and put it in the trolley. It can be a mushroom, a different colour of pepper or a bag of red lentils.

One new plant per shop is not much, and that is the point. A new plant only becomes an ordinary one when it comes back to the table, and then the child's tasting count starts on its own. The household is not eating more, it is eating more kinds.

## Your first week

Start small. The first week is a measuring week, not a challenge.

1. **Days 1 to 3: see what is already there.** Note which plants reach the table. It is usually more than you expect.
2. **Day 4: one new plant.** Pick one that fits what you already cook and put it next to the plate.
3. **Days 5 to 7: keep counting.** Put the new plant out again, without comment, and see what happens.
4. **Sunday: one look.** How many different plants this week. That number is your starting point, not a mark.

A household that lands on 14 this week and 17 next week has nothing to correct. A week that comes in lower than the last is a week with many of the same meals, and that happens to everyone.

## What backfires

- **Counting as a chore.** If the number becomes one person's job, or a moment everyone dreads, drop it. One tap after the meal is enough.
- **A child's plate as a score.** A child who hears that their plate counts towards a number gains a reason to refuse. The tasting count stays small and theirs, the household count stays with the adults.
- **Comparing siblings.** "Look, your sister had four." Nobody learns to like a food in order to beat a brother or sister.
- **Cooking twice.** A separate meal for the child so the household reaches its number works against the tasting count. One pan, several kinds, and everyone takes what they take.
- **The number as a verdict.** A week of 12 plants is not a failed week. Do not say it that way, not even to yourself.

## If you are worried about your child's eating

A counting game is for ordinary weeks. Talk to your doctor if your child is losing weight or not growing, if whole groups of food are missing, if they gag or panic at new food, or if the worry is getting too big for you. There is help for that, and it fits the situation better than any count.

## How Project Food does this

Project Food is an app on your phone for everyone at your table. Every plant is a card, and a card fills by tasting. After a meal you tap what was on the table, per person. The family ring shows how many different plants the household has had this week, and each plant's card moves up to silver at 5 tastes and to gold at 10. The catalogue holds 224 plants (checked 2026-09-24), herbs and spices included. The app gives no advice on weight or nutrition and promises nothing for a child. It is a counter, and something to talk about at the table.

## To remember

- 30 plants a week is a guideline from research in adults, not a promise and not a test.
- Each kind counts once a week. Variety beats quantity.
- The household has one count. A lick from your child counts.
- A child is never measured against 30. Their game is the tasting count.
- One new plant per shop is enough.
- Counting as a chore, a plate as a score and comparing siblings backfire.
- Weight loss, whole groups refused, gagging or panic: talk to your doctor.
