30 plants a week sounds like a project until you look at one Tuesday. The pasta night alone is 4 or 5 plants: wheat, tomato, onion, garlic, basil. Nobody has ever counted it that way, and it is mildly unsettling how quickly the number adds up once you do. This is the family version of the guideline: what 30 plants a week means, what counts, how a household shares one count, and how to start a first week without anyone being left behind.

## What 30 plants a week means

The guideline comes from the American Gut Project, a large citizen-science study in which adults who ate more than 30 different plants a week had a more varied gut flora than adults who ate 10 or fewer (McDonald, 2018). It is an observation in adults, based on what people reported eating. It says nothing about what the number does for a child, and we do not claim it does.

What the number is, is a useful anchor. It points at variety rather than volume. A household that rotates the same five vegetables sits at around 10 plants. The same household with one new legume and a handful of seeds gets a good deal further, without a single portion getting bigger. That is why we call it the guideline and not a target you hit or miss.

## What counts as a plant

A plant is a plant food you can name. There are 8 kinds: fruit, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, herbs and spices. Each plant counts once a week, however often you eat it. Apple on Monday and Wednesday is 1 plant, apple and pear is 2.

The rule is generous on purpose. The point is variety, not bookkeeping. Coffee, a potato, a spoonful of ketchup, a pinch of cumin: most of the questions parents ask have a short and friendly answer. The full list, with every borderline case in one place, is in the article on [what counts as a plant](/en/learn/plant-diversity/what-counts-as-a-plant).

## Counting 30 plants a week as a household

In a household everyone shares one count. You count per plant, not per person. If you eat the lentils and your partner does too, that is 1 plant. If your child only licks the lentils, that counts as well, because the household has had them. Nobody has to eat their own 30.

That makes the number reachable in a way it is not for one person alone. Four people each clearing a plate cover far more different plants than one person trying hard. One eats the olives, another the cucumber, a third tastes the parsley. The list grows and nobody has to treat their plate as a score.

How a whole week with four eaters gets to 30 plants without cooking twice gets its own article.

## The tasting count belongs to the child

There are two counts, and it helps to keep them apart. The household count belongs to the grown-ups: 30 plants a week, together. The tasting count belongs to the child: how many times has this been tasted, on the way to the day it is liked. Most children need 8 to 15 tastes of a new food before they accept it, sometimes more. A lick counts.

So a child is never measured against 30. Their game is smaller and closer: 2 more tastes and the card for that plant levels up. Everything they taste feeds the household count, but the pressure sits only with the adults, and there it is a cheerful number. How to keep the tasting count going without it becoming a project is in the article on [how many times to try a food](/en/learn/learn-to-eat-everything/how-many-times-to-try-a-food).

## What a normal week looks like

You do not have to cook anything unusual. A week of ordinary meals gets closer than you expect, because every meal has several plants in it.

- **Breakfast.** Porridge oats with an apple, a spoon of seeds and cinnamon: 4 plants.
- **Lunch.** Bread with cucumber and tomato, a handful of nuts alongside: 4 or 5 plants, depending on the bread.
- **Pasta with tomato sauce.** Wheat, tomato, onion, garlic and basil: 5 plants.
- **Soup or stew.** Lentils, carrot, celery, onion and parsley: 5 plants, one of them already counted.
- **Rice with chicken and vegetables.** Rice, pepper, broccoli and ginger: 4 plants.
- **Snacks.** A satsuma, a banana, popcorn: 3 plants.

That is an example, not a prescription. Add up a week of meals like these, take out the repeats, and you land somewhere between 18 and 25. The last few come from one new plant at the shop, a herb in the wok, or a different pulse on Saturday.

## Why variety does more than quantity

The number counts plants, not portions. That changes how you look at a week. A second plate of broccoli on Thursday adds nothing to the count, while a first spoonful of black beans adds 1. For a household that is a relief: nobody needs to eat more, you only need to put something different on the table now and then.

It also works the way children learn. A child who gets the same three vegetables every day has few chances to meet anything new. A child who finds one small new plant beside the plate each week builds their list slowly, at their own pace. The household's variety and the child's tasting count pull the same way.

## The shopping rule

The easiest way to grow the list is one question at the shop: which plant have we not had in 3 weeks. That is the whole rule. Pick that one and put it in the trolley. It can be a mushroom, a different colour of pepper, or a bag of red lentils.

One new plant per shop is not much, and that is the point. A new plant becomes an ordinary plant when it comes back to the table a few times, and that is when the child's tasting count starts running by itself. The household does not eat more. It eats a wider range.

## Your first week

Start small. The first week is a measuring week, not a challenge.

1. **Days 1 to 3: see what is already there.** Note which plants reach the table. There are usually more than you think.
2. **Day 4: one new plant.** Pick one that fits something you cook anyway, and put it beside the plate.
3. **Days 5 to 7: keep counting.** Put the new plant on the table again, without comment, and see what happens.
4. **Sunday: one look.** How many different plants this week. That number is your starting point, not a grade.

A household that lands on 14 this week and 17 the next has nothing to correct. The guideline points in a direction. A week that comes in lower than the last is just a week with a lot of the same meals, and that happens in every home.

## What backfires

- **Counting as a chore.** If the count becomes one person's job, or a fixed moment everyone dreads, it stops. One tap after the meal is enough.
- **A child's plate as a score.** A child who hears that their plate counts towards a number has been handed a reason to refuse. The tasting count stays small and theirs, the household count stays with the adults.
- **Comparing siblings.** "Look, your sister had four." Nobody learns to like a food by beating a brother or sister.
- **Cooking twice.** A separate meal for the child so that the household reaches its number works against the tasting count. One pan, several plants, and everyone takes what they take.
- **The number as a verdict.** A week of 12 plants is not a failed week. Do not say it that way, not even to yourself.

## When to ask for help

A counting game is meant for ordinary weeks. Talk to your doctor if your child is losing weight or not growing, if whole groups of food are missing, if they gag or panic around new food, or if the worry has grown bigger than you can carry on your own. There is help for that, and it fits that situation better than any counter.

## How Project Food does this

Project Food is an app on your phone for counting plants, alone or with everyone at your table. Each plant is a card, and a card fills by tasting. After a meal you tap what was on the table, per person. The household ring shows how many different plants you have had this week, and each card levels up at 1, 5 and 10 tastes. The catalogue holds 224 plants (checked 2026-09-24), herbs and spices included. The app gives no advice about weight or nutrition and promises nothing for a child. It is a counter, and something to talk about at the table.

## To remember

- 30 plants a week is a guideline from research in adults, not a promise and not a test.
- Each plant counts once a week. Variety does more than quantity.
- A household has one count. A lick from your child counts towards it.
- A child is never measured against 30. Their game is the tasting count.
- One new plant per shop is enough.
- Counting as a chore, a plate as a score and comparing siblings backfire.
- Weight loss, whole groups refused, gagging or panic: talk to your doctor.
