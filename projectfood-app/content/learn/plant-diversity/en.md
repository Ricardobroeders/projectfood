30 plants a week sounds like a project until you look at a single dinner. Tuesday's pasta already brings in 4 or 5: wheat, tomato, onion, garlic, basil. Nobody has ever looked at it that way, including whoever cooked it. This is the family version of the guideline: what 30 plants a week means, what counts, how everyone at the table shares one count, and how to start a first week without anyone being left behind.

## What 30 plants a week means

The guideline comes from the American Gut Project, a large citizen science study in which adults who ate more than 30 different plants a week had a more varied gut flora than adults who ate 10 or fewer (McDonald, 2018). It is an observation in adults, based on what people reported eating. It says nothing about what the number does for a child, and we do not say it either.

What the number is, is a handy anchor. It points at variety rather than volume. A household that rotates the same five vegetables sits at around 10 plants. The same household with one new legume and a handful of seeds gets a good way further, without any single portion growing. That is why we call it a guideline, not a target you hit or miss.

## What counts as a plant

A plant is a plant food you can name. There are eight kinds: fruit, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, seeds, herbs and spices. Each kind counts once a week, however often you eat it. Apple on Monday and Wednesday is 1 plant, apple and pear is 2.

The rule is generous on purpose. It is about variety, not bookkeeping. Coffee, a potato, a spoonful of ketchup, a pinch of cumin: most of the questions parents ask have a short, kind answer. The full list, with every borderline case in a row, is in the article on [what counts as a plant](/en/learn/plant-diversity/what-counts-as-a-plant).

## Counting together as a family

A household shares one count. You count by kind of plant, not by person. If you eat the lentils and so does your partner, that is 1 plant. If your child only licks the lentils, that counts too, because the household has had them. Nobody has to eat their own 30.

That makes the number reachable in a way it is not for one person alone. Four people who each finish their own plate cover far more kinds than one person trying hard. One eats the olives, another the cucumber, a third tries the parsley. The list grows without anyone having to treat their plate as a score.

How a whole week with four at the table reaches 30 plants without cooking twice gets its own article.

## The tasting count is the child's

There are two counts, and it helps to keep them apart. The household count belongs to the grown-ups: 30 plants a week, together. The tasting count belongs to the child: how many times they have tasted something on the way to liking it. Most children need 8 to 15 tastes of a new food before they accept it, sometimes more. A lick counts.

So a child is never measured against 30. Their game is smaller and closer: 2 more tastes and the card for that plant levels up. Everything they taste feeds the household count, but the pressure stays with the adults, and for them it is a cheerful number. How to keep the tasting count without turning it into a project is in the guide to [how many times to try a food](/en/learn/learn-to-eat-everything/how-many-times-to-try-a-food).

## What an ordinary week looks like

You do not need to cook anything special. A week of normal meals gets closer than you would think, because every meal is made of several kinds of plant.

- **Breakfast.** Porridge oats with an apple, a spoonful of seeds and cinnamon: 4 plants.
- **Lunch.** Bread with cucumber and tomato and a handful of nuts: 4 or 5 plants, depending on the bread.
- **Pasta with tomato sauce.** Wheat, tomato, onion, garlic and basil: 5 plants.
- **Soup or stew.** Lentils, carrot, celery, onion and parsley: 5 plants, of which the onion is already counted.
- **Rice with chicken and vegetables.** Rice, pepper, broccoli and ginger: 4 plants.
- **Between meals.** A clementine, a banana, popcorn: 3 plants.

That is an example, not a prescription. Add up a week of meals like these, remove the repeats, and you land somewhere between 18 and 25. The last few come from one new plant in the shop, a herb in the wok or a different legume on Saturday.

## Why variety beats quantity

The number counts kinds, not portions. That changes how you look at a week. A second helping of broccoli on Thursday adds nothing to the count, while a first spoonful of black beans adds 1. For a household that is a relief: nobody has to eat more, you only have to put something different on the table now and then.

It works the way children learn, too. A child who gets the same three vegetables every day has few chances to meet anything new. A child who finds one small new kind beside the plate each week builds their list slowly, at their own pace. The household's variety and the child's tasting count pull in the same direction.

## The shopping rule

The easiest way to grow the list is one question in the shop: which plant have we not had for three weeks. That is the whole rule. Pick that one and put it in the trolley. It can be a mushroom, a different colour of pepper or a bag of red lentils.

One new plant at a time is not much, and that is the point. A new plant only becomes an ordinary one when it keeps coming back to the table, and that is when the child's tasting count starts running by itself. The household does not eat more, it eats more widely.

## Your first week

Start small. The first week is a measuring week, not a challenge.

1. **Days 1 to 3: see what is already there.** Note the plants that reach the table. There are usually more than you expect.
2. **Day 4: one new plant.** Choose one that sits close to what you already cook and put it beside the plate.
3. **Days 5 to 7: keep counting.** Put the new plant out again, without comment, and see what happens.
4. **Sunday: one look.** How many different plants this week. That number is your starting point, not a mark.

A household that reaches 14 this week and 17 the next has nothing to correct. The guideline points in a direction. A week that comes in lower than the last is simply a week with a lot of the same meals, and that happens to everyone.

## What backfires

- **Counting as a chore.** If the number becomes one person's job, or a fixed moment everyone dreads, drop it. One tap after dinner is enough.
- **A child's plate as a score.** A child who hears that their plate counts towards a number has one more reason to refuse. The tasting count stays small and theirs, the household count stays with the adults.
- **Comparing siblings.** "Look, your sister had four." Nobody learns to like a food in order to beat a brother or sister.
- **Cooking twice.** A separate meal for the child so the household reaches its number works against the tasting count. One pot, several kinds of plant, and everyone takes what they want.
- **The number as a verdict.** A week of 12 plants is not a failed week. Do not say it that way, not even to yourself.

## If you are worried about how your child eats

A counting game is meant for ordinary weeks. Talk to your doctor if your child is losing weight or not growing, if whole food groups are missing, if they gag or panic at new food, or if the worry is getting too big for you. There is help for that, and it fits the situation better than any count.

## How Project Food does this

Project Food is an app on your phone, for you and everyone who eats at your table. Every plant is a card, and a card fills by tasting. After a meal you tap what was on the table, person by person. The family ring shows how many different plants you have had this week, and each plant's card levels up at 1, 5 and 10 tastes. The catalogue has 224 plants (checked 2026-09-24), herbs and spices included. The app gives no advice on weight or nutrition and promises nothing for a child. It is a counter, and something to talk about at the table.

## To remember

- 30 plants a week is a guideline from research in adults, not a promise and not an exam.
- Each kind of plant counts once a week. Variety beats quantity.
- A household has one count. A lick from your child counts.
- A child is never measured against 30. Their game is the tasting count.
- One new plant per shop is enough.
- Counting as a chore, a plate as a score and comparing siblings all backfire.
- Weight loss, whole groups refused, gagging or panic: talk to your doctor.
