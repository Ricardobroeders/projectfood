Never miss twice is the only rule that matters after a bad eating day. One miss is an accident; it leaves the habit where it was. Two in a row is a pattern, and the pattern is a new habit of not doing the thing. The rule comes from James Clear's Atomic Habits, the data behind it is older, and it belongs in the guide to [healthy eating habits that stick](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits) because it is where most of those habits actually end. Not at the pizza. At the day after the pizza.

## Missing once is an accident

Everyone misses. A birthday, a late train, a week of illness, a day where dinner was crisps on the sofa and nothing else. The question is not whether this happens but what it does to the habit, and the answer from the data is reassuring. In the study that tracked 96 adults building a daily habit over 12 weeks, a single missed day made no material difference to how automatic the habit became (Lally, 2010). The weeks of repetition before the miss are still there the next morning.

So a miss is an event, not a verdict. Nothing has been lost that was not lost by every other person who ever kept a habit.

## Why twice is different

The second day is where the habit changes hands. One miss followed by a normal day is a normal week. One miss followed by another is a new routine: the routine of not doing it, with its own cue (I skipped yesterday), its own reasoning (the week is ruined anyway) and its own reward (nothing to do tonight). That routine forms as readily as the one you wanted, and on the same timeline.

The rule, then, is not "never miss", which nobody keeps and which turns every miss into a failure. It is "never miss twice", which anyone can keep, because it asks for exactly one ordinary day after the bad one.

## The next meal, not Monday

Monday is the enemy of the rule. "I will start again on Monday" sounds like a plan and works as permission: permission to miss Thursday, Friday, Saturday and Sunday, which turns one miss into five and five into the end. The same goes for "next month", "after the holiday" and "once things calm down".

The habit does not live in the calendar. It lives in the next meal, and the next meal is in a few hours. The restart is one taste of one plant at that meal, nothing more. Not a perfect day, not a fresh plan, not a clean slate. The smallest true version of the habit, done once, so that the second day is a day you did it.

## A freeze, not a reset

This is where the design of a tracker decides whether the rule can be kept. A streak that resets to zero on one missed day punishes precisely the event the data says is harmless. After a reset, the honest arithmetic is that everything is lost and nothing is gained by continuing, and people act on that arithmetic by stopping.

A streak with a free pass does the opposite. The missed day is absorbed, the count stays where it was, and the only thing the tracker asks is that you get back at the next meal. That is the rule, built into the tool. The reasoning behind the number of weeks a habit needs, and why the middle weeks are the fragile ones, is in [how long it takes to form a healthy eating habit](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits/how-long-to-form-a-healthy-eating-habit).

## What a streak is for

A streak is not a score. It is a way of making the smallest true thing visible every day, so that the week has a result when the mirror does not. Read that way, a long streak is nice and a short one is fine, and neither is a judgement on the day.

Three things make a streak serve the habit instead of the other way round:

- It counts the smallest true thing: a day with one taste is a day.
- It forgives a single miss, because a single miss is harmless.
- It never shows what you did not do.

## What guilt does to a habit

Guilt feels like a motivator and works as a brake. A day that ends in "I was bad" makes the next attempt feel like a trial, and trials get postponed. The clean-plate table most of us grew up at taught this lesson well: eating is a thing you can fail at. It is not. There are days with a taste and days without, and the only interesting question is what happens at the next meal.

If the guilt is doing more than that, if eating has become a source of fear, secrecy or distress, or most days end in it, that is a reason to talk to your doctor rather than to a tracker.

## The smallest restart

After a bad day, the restart that works is embarrassingly small. One plant you do not usually eat, next to whatever is on the plate, at the next meal. A handful of frozen peas counts. A herb on the eggs counts. The ideas for making that one taste effortless are in [how to eat more vegetables every day](/en/learn/healthy-eating-habits/eat-more-vegetables-every-day).

## How Project Food does this

Project Food is an app on your phone, for you alone or for everyone at your table. The streak counts days with at least one plant logged, and a missed day uses a free pass before the streak ends, so one bad day costs nothing. After the last meal it asks what you tasted, you tap the plants, every new one becomes a card, and nothing in it shows what you did not eat. The next meal is always the restart.

## Worth remembering

- One miss is an accident. The habit is intact.
- Two in a row is a new habit, of not doing it.
- The restart is the next meal, never Monday.
- The restart is one taste, never a perfect day.
- A streak with a free pass keeps the rule; a reset to zero breaks it.
- Leave the guilt out. It brakes, it does not drive.
