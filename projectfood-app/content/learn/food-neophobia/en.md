Food neophobia is the reluctance to eat something a child does not recognise, and it is normal between the ages of 2 and 6. It explains most of what happens at dinner: why a toddler refuses a new food before tasting it, why repetition works and why persuasion does not. The verdict arrives before the fork does. How to handle it at the table is in the guide on [how to get kids to eat vegetables](/en/learn/learn-to-eat-everything); this page explains the word itself.

## What food neophobia is

Neophobia means fear of the new. Applied to food, it describes a refusal that arrives before the tasting: a piece gets pushed away on sight, sometimes before the plate has properly landed. That order is the giveaway, because the flavour was never given a turn.

It is a stage of development, not a disorder. There is no diagnosis behind it and no treatment that goes with it. Researchers use the term to separate this normal phase, which touches nearly every child, from feeding difficulties that do need attention (Dovey, 2008).

## How it shows up at the table

You recognise it by the sequence: refusal first, food maybe later. A piece gets moved away on sight, "I don't like it" arrives for something that has never been near the mouth, and sometimes the plate is turned down before it is even set down properly.

A preference for predictability usually comes with it: food that must not touch, the same bowl, the same order, the same shape of sandwich. A food that was fine last week and gets refused now because it was cut differently belongs here too. That is not stubbornness, it is the same caution extended to how something looks.

## The age: strongest from 2 to 6

For most children it begins around the second birthday and peaks between 2 and 6 (Dovey, 2008). It fades after that, sooner in some children than others, and a small trace of it often lingers into adulthood.

Babies show almost none of it. That explains the pattern so many parents recognise: a child who finished every jar of puree and, around their second birthday, will suddenly eat only bread, pasta and yoghurt. Nothing changed in between. The stage simply started.

## Why it exists

The common explanation is that caution became useful right when a child started moving independently. A toddler who can walk and grab can also put something in their mouth that should not be there. Suspicion of unfamiliar food is not a fault at that age, it is a safeguard, and bitterness is the clearest alarm, because many poisonous plants taste bitter.

Age adds to it. Around the second year a child discovers that saying no has an effect, and the plate is one of the few places where that works immediately. The two reinforce each other, and together they make up the stretch you are living through now.

## Food neophobia versus picky eating

The two get confused and they are not the same thing. Food neophobia is about new food, refused before it is tasted. A child who also turns down food they already know and have eaten before is doing something else.

In practice the two show up in the same child, but the approach differs. For neophobia, offering the same food calmly and often is the whole answer. If the plate has narrowed to the same handful of foods for months, that is a different pattern, and the referral guidance below still applies if weight, growth or the number of accepted foods becomes a worry.

## What helps

Repetition, in small amounts, without pressure. A child likes a food more the more often they have tasted it, and in two-year-olds that link has been measured directly (Birch, 1982). In a home trial with parents of children aged 2 to 6, two weeks of one small daily taste was enough for most children to start liking the vegetable (Wardle, 2003).

In practice that comes down to four things. Offer the same new food every day, in a small piece, on its own saucer next to the plate. Eat it yourself and say nothing about it. Accept whatever happens, including a lick, a sniff or leaving it alone. And keep going longer than feels sensible, 8 to 15 times, sometimes more. How to count without turning it into a project is in the article on [how many times a child needs to try a new food](/en/learn/learn-to-eat-everything/how-many-times-to-try-a-food).

## What makes it worse

- **Pressure and pushing.** A child who feels forced links the tension to the food and remembers that longer than the taste.
- **Finishing the plate.** It turns a new piece into a risk instead of a chance.
- **Something different every night.** A new food every evening resets the repetition to zero.
- **Cooking a separate meal.** Unfamiliar food never comes past the kitchen then, and the stage lasts longer than it needs to.
- **Talking about it a lot.** The more attention a piece gets, the heavier it becomes.

## Can it be prevented

Not the stage itself. Food neophobia belongs to development and turns up in nearly every child, including children who ate everything as a baby and children of parents who did everything right. That is the reassuring part: no earlier choice caused this.

What you do affect is how long it lasts and how heavy it feels. A child who regularly meets something new without an argument attached moves through it faster than a child for whom every new food becomes a negotiation. So keep offering, especially in the stretch where it feels pointless.

## When to talk to your doctor

There is one point where this stops being an ordinary stage. Talk to your doctor if your child is losing weight or not growing, eats fewer than about 20 different foods, refuses whole groups such as all vegetables or all fruit, gags or panics at new food, or if the worry is getting too big for you as a parent. That is a different situation from food neophobia, and there is help for it.

## How Project Food keeps the years visible

Project Food is an app on your phone for the whole family. After dinner you tap what each person tasted, and every taste counts towards that plant's card, silver at 5 tastes, gold at 10. In a stage where the plate shows little, a count that keeps rising is often the only evidence that something is moving.

## Worth remembering

- Food neophobia is the refusal of new food on sight, strongest between 2 and 6.
- It is a stage, not a disorder, and it passes for almost every child.
- What helps: the same small taste, often, without pressure, while you eat it too.
- What makes it worse: forcing, the clean plate, a new food every night, cooking twice, talking about it.
- Weight loss, fewer than 20 foods, gagging or panic at new food: talk to your doctor.
