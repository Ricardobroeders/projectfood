# Research notes — what customers want, keep and pay for (2026-10)

**What it is:** Ricardo's question on 2026-10-11, while the closed test runs: what are customers
of food-logging apps (ZOE and its field) and of family and kids-eating apps looking for, what do
they really like once they use it, and what makes them pay or leave. Three passes the same day:
adult food-logging apps (ZOE, Yuka, Noom, MyFitnessPal, Lifesum, Yazio, Cronometer, Ate, the
30-plants trackers, Oura and Whoop for the score angle); family and kid apps (Yummi, EatPal, Food
Explorer Club, Food Hopper, Playful Eats, Teach Your Monster, Tiny Tastes, Solid Starts, Kids Eat
in Color, Joon, OurHome, Homey, S'moresUp, Habitica, Finch); and the evidence base (RevenueCat and
Adapty benchmarks, systematic reviews on nutrition-app attrition, the streak literature,
willingness to pay). Input to the brainstorm "benefits worth paying for" (item 28,
[[strategy-backlog]]) and to rows 1, 4, 9 and 10. Prices and ratings are as seen on 2026-10-11
unless dated otherwise.

## The headline

People pay for a nutrition app when it gives them one of three things: an **outcome they can
see today** (a score, a count, a record), **ideas that fill a gap** they did not know they had,
or **authority at a worried moment** (Solid Starts, Noom's lessons). They keep it as long as
logging stays effortless and the app keeps saying something new. They leave over **billing
surprises** first, **price against a thinning value** second, and **novelty fading** third:
by year two ZOE is "essentially a food logging app", Joon's pet bores the child in weeks,
Habitica is dropped because the habit stuck. Nobody in any source calls streaks, badges,
community feeds or AI chat worth paying for. The picky-eater app category is thin (most apps
under 100 ratings, a $15-a-month meal planner with one review), so for families the evidence
is directional, not statistical.

Not praised by users anywhere, though claimed by apps: community feeds, leaderboards, AI chat.
A best-worst scaling study of gamification elements (220 respondents, HICSS 2021) ranks goals,
progress graphs, progress bars, rewards and levels highest, and leaderboards, badges, narratives
and social interaction lowest; about five elements is the optimum.

## 2. Why they cancel

Ranked by how many apps show it, all passes.

1. **Billing surprise and lock-in.** Dominant on every Trustpilot page: silent annual renewal,
   lump sums, no refund, hard to cancel (ZOE "at no point did I understand it was a subscription",
   Noom "$200 renewal without notice", Joon, Greenlight, Whoop). In 72,084 Google Play reviews of
   diet trackers the lowest-rated topic is subscription charges and cancellation (2.32 of 5).
   Solid Starts: "paying $130 up front for a year is too much. I'd rather pay $11 a month".
2. **Price against value, and paywalling what used to be free.** 29% of MyFitnessPal negatives,
   33% of Lifesum's; "used to be free" in 15% of Lifesum's since 2025. Clove once capped free
   logging at three foods a day and the reviews still mention it.
3. **Ads in the free tier.** 27% of all calorie-tracker negatives. We have none.
4. **Novelty fades.** ZOE in year two: "committed to another 12 months of what is essentially a
   food logging app"; Noom's lessons "repetitive after initial weeks"; Yazio "hasn't changed much
   in years"; Whoop's extreme visuals "tiresome"; Joon's pet fades in weeks to a few months; Teach
   Your Monster "players stay in one scene without leveling up". The Dutch study below names the
   remedy: continuously new information and new personal goals.
5. **Setup burden and clutter** (family apps above all): OurHome "very busy and overly
   complicated", S'moresUp "overwhelming with so many options", Food Explorer Club's reward setup
   "can take some time", Habitica "just one more thing to do everyday".
6. **Bugs, sync, data loss, a broken streak.** MyFitnessPal's 1,465-day streak lost to a glitch;
   Finch users losing a two-year streak to data loss; OurHome logouts.
7. **Guilt.** Both systematic reviews report that logging "unhealthy" food produces guilt and a
   calorie focus "can easily become an obsession". The plant count has no such entry.
8. **Mission accomplished.** Habitica dropped after five months because "nothing more to do"
   once the habit stuck. For a tasting app this is the kid who eats fine now, which is the
   outcome we promise. The answer has to be a second reason to stay (the collection as a record,
   the adult's own 30, the season), not a reason to keep needing help.

## 3. What they call worth paying for

In the users' own words, across apps:

- **Unlimited history and trends** (Cronometer's free tier keeps 7 days; Oura's charts and
  weekly reports; Yuka's history). The "over time" view is a paid feature people name.
- **Suggestions that fill gaps** (Cronometer Oracle, Plant Points).
- **Removing ads** (Cronometer Gold: "worth it to get rid of the ads"). Not available to us as a
  lever, and better so.
- **Offline and text search** (Yuka).
- **Supporting an independent maker** (Yuka's pay-what-you-want, published accounts).
- **A parent approval step** so the child cannot self-mark (Joon's essential feature; ours is
  "the tap is yours, the card is theirs" by design).
- **A second caregiver and more children in sync** (S'moresUp, Homey, EatPal's tiering). The
  second adult on a household is a candidate paid line we had not listed.
- Not worth it in their words: $15 to $25 a month meal planning (EatPal, one review), coaching
  content "I learned for free on Instagram" (BetterBites), annual-only pricing.

Reading against [[concept-business-model]]: €39.99 a year sits on the category median; €3.99 a
month is under half the category's monthly ($9.99) and equal to its weekly price. The niche
30-plants trackers charge $12.99 to $49.99 a year, most under $15 or one-off; family apps a
parent pays for charge $60 to $100 a year (Joon, Solid Starts, Playful Eats). So the solo adult
compares us with a $15 tracker and the household with an $80 app. One price has to carry both,
or the paid plan has to be the household and insight layer the solo adult mostly does not need.

Hard paywalls earn more per install (and Adapty measures 21% higher LTV) but the class network
and the kid's free collection rule them out for us ([[concept-business-model]] option C,
rejected). The freemium path is the harder one and the benchmarks say so: 2% to 3% of installs.

## 5. Streaks, scores and the invisible outcome

- **Repairable beats punitive.** Silverman and Barasch (J. Consumer Research 2023, seven
  studies): about 66% continued a behaviour when the logged streak was intact against 58% when
  shown broken, and the effect "attenuated when consumers can repair a broken streak". Sharif
  and Shu (2017): goals with emergency reserves (seven days with two skips allowed) are preferred
  and performed better than easy or rigid goals. Finch's rule, "your bird never dies, streaks
  never punish you", has multi-year users. Our free freeze ([[concept-retention-loop]]) is the
  right design; a reset to zero is the wrong one, and we have said so publicly in the never-miss-
  twice article.
- **Duolingo's own data** (company A/B tests): one lesson extends the streak (+3.3% day-14
  retention); milestone animations +1.7% day-7; a 7-day streak makes the next day 2.4× more
  likely; "streaks can't make up for a weak product"; letting users set their own streak goal
  raised retention. Retention "is most fragile in the first seven days".
- **Scores.** No academic evidence that a composite score improves retention; the product
  evidence (ZOE, Oura, Lifesum) is that people watch a number with written reasons behind it.
  Whoop shows the failure mode: a score people stop trusting, drawn too loud.
- **A vegetable-intake app RCT** (97 young adults, 2019): gamification without social support had
  the highest first-week attrition; neither gamification nor social features changed logging,
  but time engaged predicted vegetable intake. Mechanics do not replace the habit loop.

## 6. Implications for Project Food

What the evidence confirms (keep, say it louder):
- Plants not calories, the free freeze, no ads, the three-step onboarding, the tap as the one
  action, never showing what you did not eat. Each matches a named benefit or avoids a named
  churn reason.
- Option A of the business model (the parent's payoff paid, the kid's loop free) is the design
  the churn data supports: never take away something in use, never paywall the reward.
- The Sunday shopper advice as the paid ritual matches benefit 4 (ideas that fill gaps).

What the evidence adds (candidates for item 28):
- **The long-horizon number.** The weekly count resets; what people pay for is history and
  trends. A per-member lifetime count ("41 plants tasted since September"), a monthly recap and
  a year view are the cheapest answer to the "no visible result" problem, before any garden.
- **Something new each week.** Novelty fade is churn reason 4 and the Dutch study's remedy is
  "continuously new information". The season rotation, the quests of item 26 and the card facts
  are our supply; the question is cadence, not more features.
- **A second adult on the household** as a paid line (benefit named in three family apps),
  alongside history and advice.
- **Billing as brand.** Renewal reminder before an annual charge, a monthly option kept, one-tap
  cancel, the founder's pay-what-you-want tone of Yuka. The cheapest retention lever in the whole
  set is not surprising anyone.
- **Trial length.** A 7-day trial sits in the category's conversion peak; a longer one (17 to 32
  days) retains better. For a habit that needs ten weeks, lean long, or no trial and a free tier
  that is whole.
- **The exit question.** The household whose kid now eats everything has no reason to stay
  unless the adult's own 30 and the collection as a family record give one. The two-ways
  decision ([[decision-2026-10-07-two-ways-to-use-it]]) is also the churn answer.

What it does not settle: whether solo adults pay at all at €39.99 (they compare with $15
trackers), whether a household plan retains better (no published data on family plans anywhere),
and whether collectible cards hold past the Joon window of four to eight weeks (no trial has
measured a card mechanic; the tasting trials behind it hold at three months).

## Contradictions and open questions

- The adult trackers are tiny (tens of ratings), so their praise is anecdote; the mass apps are
  calorie counters, a different job. Our closest evidence is the closed test itself.
- Reddit was unreachable from the research environment; its sentiment arrives second-hand via
  review-mining blogs and is marked so.
- Joon's "fades in 4 to 8 weeks" comes from competitors; the reviews say "a few months".
- Teach Your Monster's "16% of children tried new foods" is the maker's own survey.
- A 2026 JMIR mHealth scoping review on engagement strategies in nutrition apps
  (mhealth.jmir.org/2026/1/e82276) was not retrieved; worth a read.
- The "78% say community makes habits easier" line (Kantar 2022) sits against the gamification
  study ranking social features last and the review evidence that nobody praises feeds. Hold
  both: people value a person who notices (a cheer), not a feed.
